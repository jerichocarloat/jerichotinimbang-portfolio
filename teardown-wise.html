Lifecycle case study · No 1

Wise moved $243 billion last year. Seven million of those customers had never sent money with them before.

That single number is the most interesting line in their annual results, and it points at a retention problem worth more than any acquisition campaign. This is an outside-in case study: what their published figures imply about the lifecycle, where I think the revenue leaks, and the three journeys I would build first.

By Jericho Tinimbang ·CRM & Lifecycle Specialist ·12 minute read

Outside-in analysis. I do not work at Wise and have no access to their data.

Why this company, and why this number

Wise reported 19 million active customers, up 21%, moving $243.5 billion across borders, up 31%. Card spend reached $43.6 billion and customer balances $39 billion. Roughly half of net revenue now comes from things other than the first transfer: interest on balances, card interchange, and other services.

Read those two facts together and a strategy appears. The business is deliberately moving from transfer fees to resident money. Interest and interchange only pay if the customer keeps a balance and uses the card, which means the goal is no longer "get the transfer." It is "get the second transfer, then the balance, then the card."

Now put the 7 million next to it. More than a third of their active base sent money with Wise for the first time this year. Every one of those people is sitting at the exact moment where lifecycle marketing decides the outcome: they either form a habit, or they go quiet and the acquisition cost is written off.

The strategic opportunity

Wise acquires customers fast and well. The gap is what happens next. 37% of active users joined this year, so the leverage is not in getting more people. That part already works. It is in turning those cohorts into customers who transfer again, hold a balance and use the card. That is a lifecycle problem, and the return on fixing it can be measured.

Revenue leakage points in the customer lifecycle

A transfer app is not a habit product. People send money when life makes them, not because they opened the app. So unless you build reasons to come back, customers drift quiet even though the acquisition number counted them. That leaves three leaks, in order:

  1. Activation funnel (first to second transaction). Repeat purchase is the single strongest predictor of long-term customer value in transactional products. Until second transaction completes, cohort lifetime value remains uncertain. This is your highest-priority conversion gate.
  2. Cross-sell readiness (transaction to balance holding). A pass-through user generates single-event fee revenue. A balance-holding user generates recurring interest, spreads, and card interchange, which means fundamentally higher wallet share. This is the premium feature adoption threshold.
  3. Churn pattern masquerading as seasonality (usage interval risk). Someone who pays a bill monthly looks dormant if you check quarterly. Someone who pays quarterly looks dormant if you check twice a year. A generic win-back email then hits healthy customers and pushes up unsubscribes. The fix is to segment by each customer's own rhythm, not by a fixed calendar window.
The third point is where most enterprise teams fail. They treat periodic users as churned and send everyone the same win-back email, which annoys good customers. The answer is to segment by how often someone actually transacts, not to send more messages.

Three journeys I would build, in priority order

For each one below: the segment as I would query it, the journey as I would build it, and how I would measure whether it worked.

01

Activation: Trigger repeat transactions while trust is highest

Activation
The strategy
First-time users given an easier path to their second transfer should repeat at a much higher rate than users left to work it out alone. Trust peaks right after a first transfer lands, and that window closes fast.
Trigger window
The customer finishes a first transfer and gets the confirmation. If no second transfer starts within 10 days, the repeat-purchase journey begins.
Offer optimization
Do not ask for another transfer. Remove the friction instead: save the recipient, show a rate-locked preview, compare the rate against alternatives. Convenience beats a discount here.
Success measurement
Repeat transaction rate (second transfer) within 60 days post-first-transaction. Compare treatment group against randomized control to isolate marketing lift.

    First transfer settlesDelivered, not just initiated. Never message on a pending transfer.
  • Day 1
    In-app + pushConfirmation that lands as reassurance, plus recipient saved for next time
  • Day 10
    EmailOne-tap repeat of the same transfer, with the current rate shown
  • Day 21
    PushOnly if a rate move makes the same transfer cheaper than at day one
  • Converts
    Second transfer initiatedExit activation, enter the balance journey below
02

Upsell: Balance-holding as premium feature adoption and wallet-share expansion

Monetization
The business shift
Wise's revenue model is shifting from transactional fees (per-transfer) to recurring revenue: interest on customer balances, card interchange, FX spreads. A customer who holds money in-app is fundamentally more valuable than a pass-through transaction.
Target segment
Users with two or more repeat transactions to the same corridor. These are proven, habitual transaction patterns, not one-time movers.
Positioning strategy
Behavioral messaging: "You've executed 3 GBP→EUR transfers in five months. Smart senders hold balances for rate optimization: convert when rates favor you, not when invoices are due." Position balance-holding as strategic money management, not as a new product feature.
Compliance guardrail
Talk about convenience and timing, never about yield or returns. That keeps the message clear of financial promotion rules.
Success metrics
Premium feature adoption rate (% holding non-zero balance 30 days post-message) and wallet-share expansion (median balance value). Measure incrementally versus control.
03

Retention: Behavioral segmentation, not calendar-based win-back

Retention
The core insight
Fintech users have heterogeneous transaction frequencies, not homogeneous ones. A monthly-payer at day 45 has genuine churn risk. A quarterly-payer at day 45 is on-rhythm. Static win-back windows create false positives (messaging healthy customers, driving unsubscribes) and false negatives (missing actual churn).
Behavioral segmentation approach
Calculate each customer's median transaction interval. Flag for win-back outreach when they exceed 1.5x their own baseline, not an arbitrary company threshold. This is dynamic, individualized churn-risk scoring.
Why this outperforms
You message high-risk-only cohorts. Monthly senders at day 45 receive win-back. Quarterly senders at day 45 receive nothing (no churn signal). Messaging efficacy rises, unsubscribe rates fall, marketing ROI improves. This is precision retention targeting.
Success metrics
Win-back conversion rate and opt-out rate, both measured incrementally vs. fixed-window control. Expect tighter audience segmentation with better response and lower churn.

The segment, as I would write it:

-- Overdue against each customer’s own rhythm, not a fixed window
WITH gaps AS (
  SELECT customer_id, transfer_id, settled_at,
         DATEDIFF(settled_at, LAG(settled_at) OVER (
           PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY settled_at)) AS gap_days
  FROM   fact_transfers
  WHERE  status = 'SETTLED'
),
rhythm AS (
  SELECT customer_id,
         PERCENTILE_APPROX(gap_days, 0.5) AS median_gap,
         COUNT(*) AS transfers,
         MAX(settled_at) AS last_settled
  FROM   gaps
  WHERE  gap_days IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY customer_id
  HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2          -- needs a rhythm to be off-rhythm
)
SELECT r.customer_id, r.median_gap, r.transfers,
       DATEDIFF(CURRENT_DATE, r.last_settled) AS days_since
FROM   rhythm r
JOIN   dim_customer c USING (customer_id)
WHERE  DATEDIFF(CURRENT_DATE, r.last_settled) > r.median_gap * 1.5
  AND  DATEDIFF(CURRENT_DATE, r.last_settled) < 365   -- quiet, not gone
  AND  c.marketing_consent = TRUE
ORDER BY r.transfers DESC;
This is the query I would run on day one. It is the same shape as the one I wrote at Maya to find dormant borrowers, with median gap replacing a fixed 90 day cutoff. If a company already has this, I would want to see the control-group design instead.

Measurement rigor: Causal inference, not correlation

Every campaign gets a randomized control group from day one, not a study bolted on afterwards. For a transfer product this is not optional. Sending is seasonal, and the people who respond were often going to send anyway. A simple before-and-after chart will show a lift either way, because rates move and demand returns on its own. Without a control group you are measuring a coincidence, not your campaign.

  • Metric specification pre-launch. Define success KPI before campaign execution, not post-hoc from observed winners. Prevents p-hacking and metric cherry-picking.
  • Adequately-sized control cohort. Minimum 10 to 15% control, sized for statistical power (80%+) to detect your assumed effect size. A token 5% control cannot distinguish real lift from noise.
  • Dual-metric optimization. Track both primary metric (repeat transaction rate, balance adoption) and guardrail metric (unsubscribe/opt-out rate). A campaign that lifts transactions 3% while raising opt-outs 20% destroys LTV, but won't appear in P&L for 6+ months.
  • Single-variable test design. Isolate one lever per test: timing, channel, or offer structure, not all three. Enables clean causal attribution.

Precedent: Mechanism validation in a parallel fintech use case

This is not theory. I have run the same play in fintech: find the dormant, high-LTV segment and build a win-back journey for it. The underlying problem is the same in lending and in payments. Occasional buying hides real churn.

Wise's customer lifecycle

High acquisition, low repeat-transaction penetration

  • 37% of active base is year-one cohort (high CAC)
  • Revenue model shifting to recurring (balances, interchange)
  • Primary leakage: activation funnel (first→second transaction)
  • Secondary opportunity: cross-sell to premium features (balance, card)
Maya financial platform (case study)

Mechanism applied to lending reactivation

  • Identified behavioral dormancy vs. actual churn via transaction frequency
  • Built dynamic behavioral segmentation and tiered offer optimization
  • Implemented multi-channel campaign (email, SMS, in-app) with offer elasticity
  • Measured against a randomized control group, with the offer capped so it pays for itself

Result: 245 reactivated borrowers drove ₱19.18M incremental lending volume and ₱1.26M in service fees (about $340K and $22K). The scale is different, but the method is the same: segment on behavior, test the offer, measure against a control group. Going from 245 people to millions changes the size of the number, not the approach.

Hypothesis falsifiability: What would invalidate this framework

The analysis above is based on inferred patterns from public financial reporting and transactional behavior logic, not internal data. This framework should be treated as a set of testable hypotheses, not conclusions. Four data points would quickly confirm or disprove core assumptions within 1 to 2 weeks of analysis:

  • Second-transfer rates, by joining cohort and by corridor. If 70% or more already send again within 60 days, activation is not the bottleneck. Move the budget to balance adoption instead.
  • Distribution of inter-transaction intervals. This tells me whether sending rhythms are real and worth segmenting, or whether I imagined them. Plot the gap in days: clear monthly and quarterly clusters mean the pattern is real. One smooth curve means it is random.
  • Premium feature penetration by repeat-purchase cohort. What share of repeat customers hold any balance, and is that share growing year on year? That tells me whether the cross-sell is real.
  • Current lifecycle message volume and engagement by journey. How many emails and texts each user already gets per month, and the opt-out rate for each journey. A high opt-out rate means that journey is aimed at the wrong people.

If the second-transaction rate is already high, my first hypothesis is wrong. Drop it and put the budget behind balance adoption instead. This step is about stating what I expect, before I look.
